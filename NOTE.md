## TL;DR

Current clinical AI treats the language model as the reasoner and bolts safety on top. The behavioral pattern produced by this choice — confident hallucinations, inconsistency across encounters, no calibrated abstention, no audit trail that survives prompt modification — is not a set of bugs to be patched. It is the architecture telling us what it cannot do.

This note proposes an alternative position: in clinical reasoning systems, the load-bearing safety properties must be enforced by a symbolic substrate, not by the behavior of learned components. The substrate is a lattice of clinical states with sound deduction operators. The learned component proposes refinements the substrate verifies. Abstention is a first-class output. Provenance is enforced by construction.

This position extends to a two-layer architecture coupled across a third temporal axis. A patient-state substrate handles per-patient reasoning under safety constraints. An institutional-state substrate handles cross-patient allocation under capacity constraints — bed availability, drug supply, scheduled resources, specialist on-call coverage. Recommendations require licensing by both substrates. Outputs come in pairs: the unconstrained-optimal action and the institutionally-actionable plan, with explicit diff between them. Both layers carry persistent, refinable, auditable state. Both layers can abstain. The architecture acknowledges that a patient-locally-correct recommendation that the institution cannot execute is not a success — it is a failure mode of the system, and the substrate is what makes that failure mode visible and addressable rather than silent and ignored. The third axis, added in v0.10.0, treats temporal evolution of evidence, guidelines, formularies, and institutional policies as a first-class architectural concern: every substrate component carries explicit version metadata and currency status, guideline transitions propagate to active care only through clinician-mediated re-review events, and silent drift in active patient care is structurally forbidden by the architecture. Clinical evidence does not stop evolving the day a system deploys; an architecture that ignores this is architecturally complicit in patient harm.

Each of the principles named below has independent published support as of May 2026. The synthesis — combining them under the abstract-interpretation framing recently demonstrated by Lattice Deduction Transformers (LDT, May 2026) — appears unclaimed. This note states the synthesis, names what would have to be built, and invites disagreement on which principles are load-bearing versus decorative.

## 1. The problem with current clinical AI

The dominant bet in clinical AI from 2024 through 2026 has been: scale a generalist language model, post-train it on medical content, ground it with retrieval, wrap it in evaluation harnesses, and the resulting system will reason like a clinician. The architecture is treated as adequate. The work is in the data, the fine-tuning, the prompting, and the guardrails.

This bet has produced impressive demonstrations and unimpressive deployments. The gap between board-examination performance and bedside utility is not closing at the rate the demonstrations suggest. The complaints from clinicians who have actually worked with these systems are consistent: hallucinated citations that look authoritative, inconsistency across encounters with the same patient, confident answers in situations a human would defer on, no graded uncertainty that maps onto clinical decision thresholds, no audit trail that survives a different phrasing of the same question.

These are not implementation defects waiting for a better fine-tune. They are the architecture being asked to do something it cannot do. State representation is not absent from AI broadly — Bayesian filters, POMDPs, probabilistic graphical models, memory-augmented transformers, retrieval-linked patient timelines, EHR event stores, and clinical knowledge graphs all maintain state. The problem is more specific: foundation-model-centered clinical deployments place state outside the reasoning core, making updates soft and prompt-mediated rather than logically enforced by the learned component itself. A transformer's forward pass, in current deployments, produces a token sequence without an enforced consistency relation to the clinical state it is supposedly reasoning over. Chain-of-thought tokens look like reasoning steps but do not carry a partial-information state that subsequent tokens are forced to be consistent with. Context is the only memory the model is required to respect, and context can be selectively attended away from.

The mismatch with clinical reasoning is structural, not stylistic. Clinical reasoning is closer to a constrained state-update problem than to a sequence of independent predictions:

> S_t = U(S_{t-1}, E_t)

where S_t is the patient's current diagnostic and treatment state, E_t is new evidence (lab, vital, imaging, finding, override), and U is an update operator that must respect validity constraints. The state at 1430 is constrained by the state at 0830. A previously eliminated diagnosis can re-enter the candidate set, but only under a named justification — a false-negative test, evolving presentation, an anchoring-bias correction, a latent confounder. The substrate's job is not to make exclusion monotonic; it is to make state transitions auditable and justified.

Current foundation-model-centered systems compute something closer to f(x_t) → y_t at each timestep — episodic prediction with the prior state held only in context. This is why the same patient seen twice can produce inconsistent recommendations, and why "the AI changed its mind for no reason" is a real complaint from clinicians using these systems today. The reversal may be epistemically valid; without an enforced provenance for the state transition, it is epistemically illegible. Medicine tolerates uncertainty. It does not tolerate unexplained reversals.

A second structural failure compounds the first. Even when patient-level reasoning is sound, current clinical AI is institutionally blind. A concrete illustration: a sepsis prognostic model identifies a patient at high 28-day mortality risk at T0 using hematological parameters available immediately from routine complete blood count — extended inflammatory parameters, immature platelet fraction, immature granulocytes. The patient-level reasoning is sound, calibrated, and grounded in evidence. The recommended action under unconstrained resources is ICU admission, broad-spectrum antibiotics within the first hour, fluid resuscitation per protocol, source control. The patient is then situated in a hospital where the ICU is at capacity with a multi-hour wait, the formulary's preferred antibiotic is on three-day supply, the microbiology lab returns cultures only once daily, and the on-call intensivist is already managing five other patients. The patient-locally correct recommendation is institutionally unexecutable. The clinician now has more work, not less — they must do the allocation reasoning manually, choosing which patient gets the available bed, which second-line antibiotic to use, whether to start empiric therapy without cultures because the lab cycle will not return results in time. The system has produced a sound recommendation that creates more work for the clinician rather than less. This is the failure mode that explains why most clinical decision support in capacity-constrained settings gets ignored within months of deployment, and it is most acute in low- and middle-income country contexts where the allocation problem is the dominant constraint, not a soft factor that adjusts the recommendation.

## 2. Why scaling does not fix this

The diagnostic from MedAbstain (Jan 2026) is the cleanest empirical evidence available: providing explicit abstention options to a language model improves abstention behavior more than scaling model size or improving prompts. The architecture is the binding constraint, not the parameter count.

The Compositional Gap diagnosis (Ingram & Merritt, Dec 2025) provides independent confirmation from an adjacent benchmark. On 302 ARC-style tasks, a 1.7M-parameter transformer achieves >80% local cell accuracy on the majority of tasks but <10% grid-level synthesis. Performance is bounded by what the authors term *architectural suitability* rather than by data or training compute. The conclusion they reach — progress requires hybrid architectures with affinity-aligned modules — is the conclusion this note is structured around.

LDT (Davis, Haller, Alfarano, Santolucito, May 2026) provides the corresponding constructive proof on a different benchmark family. An 800K-parameter LDT solves Sudoku-Extreme at 100% accuracy with 100% empirical soundness, while frontier LLMs score 0%. The mechanism that produces this gap is not scale and not training data; it is the externalization of partial-information state into an explicit lattice that the model is forced to commit to between forward passes. The model's job is to propose sound refinements to the lattice. The lattice's job is to make unsound refinements impossible.

The general principle this points at: a system in which the safety-critical properties are enforced architecturally can outperform a system one to two orders of magnitude larger that enforces those properties only behaviorally. *[generated]* This is not a claim about Sudoku. It is a claim about the relationship between architectural commitments and behavioral guarantees in any domain where soundness matters more than fluency.

Clinical reasoning is exactly such a domain.

## 3. The principle, stated plainly

The diagnostic claim, testable empirically:

**Clinical AI is bottlenecked less by model size than by the absence of explicit, auditable, reversible belief-state architectures that can update medical hypotheses under hard safety constraints.** *[proposed]*

This is falsifiable. The prediction is that scaling a clinical LLM by 10× without changing its architecture will not materially improve abstention failures, audit-trail gaps, or unexplained reversal behavior. The MedAbstain finding cited in Section 2 is one piece of evidence in this direction; more is needed. If the prediction is wrong, this note is wrong.

The architectural commitment that follows:

**The clinical reasoning system's safety properties must be enforced by its substrate, not by the behavior of its learned components.** *[proposed]*

This commitment extends across two coupled substrate layers and a third temporal axis. The patient-state substrate enforces safety properties for per-patient reasoning. The institutional-state substrate enforces feasibility properties for cross-patient allocation. Both substrates carry persistent, refinable, auditable state. Both can abstain. The learned components — clinical refinement proposers, demand forecasters, queue dynamics predictors — propose moves that both substrates must license. Recommendations are produced only when patient-level reasoning is sound and institutional-level allocation is feasible; when they diverge, the system produces both outputs with explicit diff. The third axis treats temporal evolution as a first-class architectural concern: every substrate component is versioned, evidence currency is tracked architecturally, guideline transitions propagate to active care only through clinician-mediated re-review events, and silent drift in active care is structurally forbidden. *[proposed]*

This is one sentence. Everything else in this note is a consequence of it.

The phrase "the substrate" needs unpacking. It is the symbolic infrastructure that carries clinical state between learned-component invocations. In current clinical LLMs, the substrate is the prompt: a string of text the model attends to, generating a new string. The string has no enforced structure, no integrity property, no logged provenance, and no operation that is guaranteed to preserve clinical invariants.

In a substrate-first architecture, the substrate is a lattice of clinical hypotheses with explicit candidate sets, formally defined deduction operators, enforced soundness on elimination, and audited provenance on every state change. The learned component is invoked to propose refinements to the lattice. The substrate accepts the proposal, rejects it as unsound, or triggers abstention if no licensed refinement applies. The learned component is constrained by the substrate at every step. The substrate is constrained by clinical ontologies, guidelines, and evidence at every step.

This is an inversion of the current paradigm. The current paradigm puts the intelligence in the model and uses the substrate (such as it is) to feed the model. The substrate-first paradigm puts the safety in the substrate and uses the model to propose moves the substrate can verify.

## 4. Architectural commitments — patient substrate, institutional substrate, interaction semantics, temporal evolution

The architecture rests on two sets of substrate principles, a set of interaction semantics between them, and a set of temporal-evolution principles that govern how substrate components change over the system's deployment lifetime. Section 4A covers the patient-state substrate principles. Section 4B covers the institutional-state substrate principles. Section 4C covers how the two substrates communicate. Section 4D covers how all substrate components evolve as clinical evidence, guidelines, and policies change. Each set is testable; each invites disagreement.

### 4A. Patient-state substrate

Five principles follow from the substrate-first commitment applied to per-patient reasoning. Each is testable; each invites disagreement.

**4A.1. Persistent partial-information state carried architecturally.** *[proposed]* The system carries a refinable representation of what is known, ruled out, and still candidate across encounters. This representation lives in the architecture, not in the prompt. A patient seen at 0800 and again at 1400 has one lattice state that has been updated between encounters, not two independent forward passes that happen to share a context window. The constraint that the 1400 state is consistent with the 0800 state is enforced by the lattice operations, not by clever prompting.

**4A.2. Justified reversible state transitions; ontology-bounded candidates.** *[proposed]* Operations on the lattice update candidate sets — eliminations, re-additions, confidence revisions — only when licensed by a named deduction operator with cited evidence. Elimination is not final; re-addition is not free. A diagnosis ruled out by a negative test can re-enter the candidate set on a justified reason: false-negative concern, evolving presentation, anchoring-bias correction, latent confounder, test-sensitivity revision. The reversal is itself a first-class deduction step with its own provenance, not an unexplained mind-change. The substrate enforces justification, not finality.

A separate boundary holds firm. The candidate sets themselves are bounded by the substrate's clinical ontology — SNOMED CT, RxNorm, LOINC, ICD-11, the institution's formulary, the test catalog. The learned component cannot propose a diagnosis outside the ontology, a medication outside the formulary, or a test outside the catalog. The substrate makes those moves architecturally impossible. Whether the ontology can itself be expanded — for novel presentations, syndromic combinations not yet codified, conditions outside current taxonomy — is a separate, named operation with its own audit semantics and authority requirements, not a back door through the learned component.

**4A.3. First-class abstention with defined escalation semantics.** *[proposed]* When the lattice cannot accommodate an input — when no licensed deduction operator applies, when the input falls outside the ontology, when the state becomes inconsistent — abstention is a valid output with explicit escalation behavior. This is not a refusal trained into the model behaviorally. It is an architectural signal that the substrate has been pushed outside its defined domain. The escalation semantics — to whom, with what context, with what urgency — are part of the substrate's specification.

**4A.4. Auditable provenance.** *[proposed]* Every elimination has a cited license: which deduction operator applied, with which evidence, from which clinical guideline or learned proposal. Every abstention has a logged trigger: which lattice condition activated it. Every clinician override is recorded as an explicit deduction step authorized under named authority. The audit trail is reconstructible because the substrate forces every state change to declare its provenance. The audit trail survives prompt modification because the substrate's state is not the prompt.

**4A.5. Learned component as constrained refinement proposer.** *[proposed]* The transformer (or whatever the learned component is) does not output decisions. It outputs proposals that the substrate evaluates against soundness, provenance, and ontology constraints. The model can be wrong about which candidate to prioritize next. It cannot commit an action that violates a substrate-enforced constraint. The safety property is not "the model has been trained not to make this error." It is "the architecture does not have a path to this error."

This commitment explicitly does not claim symbolic supremacy. Some clinical intelligence is irreducibly learned and probabilistic: imaging pattern recognition, pathology morphology, ICU trajectory forecasting, rare phenotype clustering, multimodal signal integration, ambiguity ranking, differential expansion, note synthesis. The substrate does not replace these. It constrains them. They propose; the substrate verifies, provenances, and bounds. The architecture this note proposes is best described as *constrained probabilistic cognition* *[coined]*: structured substrate for safety, learned models for ambiguity, with the integration designed so that learned components can be wrong without being unsafe.

### 4B. Institutional-state substrate

Five principles follow from extending the substrate-first commitment to cross-patient allocation. They mirror the patient-state principles in form but operate on different state and with different operators.

**4B.1. Persistent institutional-state representation carried architecturally.** *[proposed]* The system carries a refinable representation of current resource availability — ICU and HDU bed counts, OR and infusion-chair slots, pharmacy inventory, microbiology and laboratory cycle times, on-call specialist availability, blood product stock, dialysis capacity. This state is not a snapshot reconstructed per query. It is a living lattice updated by capacity-update operators as patients are admitted, discharged, transferred, or as supplies arrive or deplete. The institutional state survives across encounters and across patients, in the same way that the patient lattice survives across encounters for one patient.

**4B.2. Justified capacity transitions; resource-bounded operations.** *[proposed]* Operations on the institutional lattice update resource state only when licensed by named capacity-update operators with cited triggers — patient admission, discharge, transfer, supply delivery, scheduled depletion, override by capacity manager. Bed counts cannot drop below zero. Antibiotic inventory cannot be allocated without provenance to a prescription. The substrate makes resource-violating moves architecturally impossible. Capacity reversals — re-opening an allocated bed because a patient was discharged early, re-availability of a drug after emergency procurement — are themselves logged operations with named justification, not silent state mutations.

**4B.3. Allocation abstention with defined escalation semantics.** *[proposed]* When the institutional substrate cannot license a patient-locally-optimal recommendation, the system produces an explicit allocation-abstention output. This is a different output type than clinical abstention: the patient-level reasoning is sound, but the institution cannot fulfill it. Escalation goes to capacity managers, not to bedside clinicians. It may trigger inter-facility transfer evaluation, queue re-prioritization with explicit ethical criteria, expedited supply procurement, or activation of surge protocols. The substrate specifies which escalations are available and under whose authority each can be invoked.

**4B.4. Auditable allocation provenance.** *[proposed]* Every capacity decision has a cited license: which queue priority applied, what trigger fired, which patient's state caused this allocation event, under whose authority the decision was made. Allocation overrides — when clinicians or administrators preempt the substrate's queue logic for clinical urgency, family request, or institutional priority — are recorded as first-class deduction steps with explicit attribution and rationale. The audit trail spans patients, not just patient-local sequences. An audit of why patient A received the available ICU bed and patient B did not is reconstructible from the substrate's logged state transitions.

**4B.5. Capacity-learned components as constrained refinement proposers.** *[proposed]* The learned components on the institutional side — demand forecasting models, queue dynamics predictors, supply availability estimators, length-of-stay predictors — do not output allocations directly. They propose refinements that the institutional substrate evaluates against feasibility, provenance, and resource constraints. The capacity-learned components can be wrong about predicted demand or anticipated discharge. They cannot allocate resources the substrate does not license. This is *constrained probabilistic allocation* *[coined]* — the institutional counterpart to constrained probabilistic cognition. The same asymmetry holds: learned components can be wrong without being unsafe, because the substrate enforces the boundary.

### 4C. Interaction semantics between substrates

The two substrates are coupled but not collapsed. Each retains its own deduction operators, its own provenance, its own abstention semantics. They communicate through a defined event interface.

**Joint licensing of recommendations.** *[proposed]* A clinical recommendation is produced only when both substrates license it. The patient substrate certifies that the reasoning is sound. The institutional substrate certifies that the action is feasible. When the two diverge — when the patient-locally-optimal action is institutionally infeasible — the system does not silently downgrade to the achievable. It produces both: the unconstrained-optimal recommendation and the institutionally-actionable plan, with explicit diff showing what is being sacrificed and why. The clinician sees both, and the override decision (accept the achievable plan, escalate for the optimal one, or invoke a third option) is itself a logged deduction step.

**Cross-layer events with defined semantics.** *[proposed]* A patient-lattice update that says "this patient needs urgent ICU care" emits an event to the institutional lattice's bed queue. The institutional lattice processes it under its own capacity-update operators and may respond with bed allocation, queue placement, or allocation abstention. An institutional-lattice update that says "no ICU bed available for six hours" emits an event to the patient-lattice prioritization across the patient population currently under reasoning. The patient lattice processes this as a constraint on its recommendation, not as a failure to act. Cross-layer events are typed, provenanced, and auditable in both directions.

**Joint abstention as a first-class output.** *[proposed]* When patient-level abstention and institutional-level abstention occur simultaneously, the system emits a joint abstention: both reasoning and allocation are outside the substrate's domain, escalate to human-only judgment. This is the most valuable output in genuinely difficult cases. It tells the clinician that no part of the system is operating with confidence and that the decision rests entirely on human judgment, with the substrate's role being only to log what was decided and by whom.

### 4D. Temporal evolution and substrate currency

Clinical evidence, guidelines, formularies, and institutional policies evolve continuously. The Surviving Sepsis Campaign updated to its 2026 edition in March 2026 (Prescott et al., Crit Care Med, doi:10.1097/CCM.0000000000007075), replacing the 2021 guidelines with 129 statements of which 46 are new. *[verified]* KDIGO published a 2024 update to its CKD guideline with substantial changes in pharmacological management (SGLT2 inhibitors, finerenone, GLP-1 RA recommendations, nephrotoxin stewardship), and the KDIGO AKI guideline is in active revision. *[verified]* Local antibiograms shift weekly. Institutional formularies update daily. An architecture that ignores this evolution is architecturally complicit in patient harm: silent staleness — the system continuing to license recommendations under operators that no longer reflect current evidence — is one of the failure modes most likely to harm patients in deployed clinical AI, precisely because the failure is invisible to clinicians who trust the system. Section 4D treats temporal evolution as a first-class architectural concern with five principles, paralleling the five-principle structure of 4A and 4B.

**Versioned substrate components.** *[proposed]* Every lattice element, deduction operator, capacity-update operator, learned component, and institutional rule carries explicit version metadata: source identity, source version with publication date, registration timestamp into the substrate, effective period, and current licensing status (active, deprecated, retired). Recommendations are licensed by the operator-version pair, not by the operator name alone; the audit trail captures both. This is structurally what HL7 FHIR's Clinical Reasoning Module already provides for PlanDefinition, ActivityDefinition, Library, and Measure resources (version, effectivePeriod, status), and what Clinical Quality Language (CQL 1.5.3 ANSI Normative, v2.0.0 in ballot) expresses for the underlying logic. *[verified]* The substrate's job is to track these and enforce that recommendations cite the specific version under which they were licensed.

**Evidence currency as first-class signal.** *[proposed]* Each operator carries source-publication date, expected update cadence based on the source's revision pattern (SSC roughly every 4–5 years; KDIGO roughly decadal for major guidelines with continuous focused updates; IDSA/ATS CAP roughly every 5–7 years; institutional formulary daily-to-weekly), and a next-check-by date. Stale operators emit explicit currency-warnings on every licensing decision. When configured currency thresholds are crossed, operators downgrade through stages: active → advisory (continues to license recommendations with explicit currency caveat) → inactive (cannot license without re-verification by a designated authority). Staleness becomes a property of the operator surfaced architecturally, not a clinician burden to remember. Operationally, this integrates with living systematic review infrastructure (Cochrane LSR methodology established 2019, MAGICapp publishing platform, Australian Living Evidence Collaboration) where the source itself is continuously updated and the substrate consumes the currency signal directly. *[verified]*

**Sound evolution under operator-set change.** *[proposed]* Adding, modifying, or retiring operators must preserve the architectural soundness guarantees. The semantics are precise. Adding a new operator: must be demonstrated not to license previously-rejected recommendations without explicit policy approval; if it does, those rejections are flagged for re-evaluation rather than silently overridden. Modifying an operator: licensing changes between operator-version N and N+1 are tracked at the recommendation level, and recommendations made under the prior version are flagged for re-evaluation under the new version. Retiring an operator: outstanding recommendations licensed under the retired operator are flagged for re-evaluation rather than silently abandoned; retirement does not erase the audit trail, it changes the operator's status and emits re-evaluation events. All operator-set changes are first-class events in the audit trail, structurally identical to schema migrations in databases or breaking-change handling in semantic versioning of APIs. The FDA's Predetermined Change Control Plan framework (final guidance Dec 2024, statutory authority FD&C Act Section 515C) and the joint FDA/Health Canada/MHRA five guiding principles for PCCPs (Focused, Risk-based, Evidence-based, Transparent, Lifecycle-oriented, Aug 2025) are the regulatory envelope this principle operates within for the learned-component portion of the substrate. *[verified]*

**Clinician-mediated propagation of change.** *[proposed]* This is the load-bearing safety claim of Section 4D. When a guideline updates and the corresponding operator is updated, currently-active recommendations licensed under the prior operator-version are surfaced to the responsible clinician as explicit re-review events. Silent updates to active care are architecturally forbidden. The choice between accepting the new operator's recommendation, continuing the current plan with documented justification, or transitioning with explicit re-evaluation rests with the clinician; the architecture surfaces the choice, logs it, and propagates the decision through the audit trail, but does not make the change silently on behalf of an unwitting clinician. This principle distinguishes substrate-first from typical electronic health record guideline updates where clinical decision support rules change in the background without clinician notification. The architectural commitment: drift in active care is impossible without explicit clinician action. The cost: high-volume care during a guideline transition generates a corresponding volume of re-review events, which the institutional substrate (4B) must accommodate as part of its capacity-management responsibilities.

**Evolution-aware provenance.** *[proposed]* The audit trail captures not just what was decided and under what operator, but under what operator-version, what evidence source the operator-version cited, what the source's currency status was at decision time, and what the propagation decision was when the operator was updated. This supports retrospective queries the architecture as previously specified could not answer: show all recommendations made under operator-version X that would now be different under operator-version X+1; show all recommendations where clinicians declined the propagation to the new version with their stated reasons; show the distribution of currency-warning levels across recommendations made in the last quarter. This is the temporal-axis extension of 4A.4 and 4B.4 (auditable provenance), and it aligns with EU AI Act Article 72's post-market monitoring requirements for high-risk AI systems (implementing-act template due February 2026; outputs feed Article 9 risk management updates and Article 73 serious incident reporting). *[verified]* The retrospective query capability is itself a safety property: institutions can detect, after the fact, whether a guideline transition produced unexpected outcome changes and whether specific propagation decisions correlated with those changes.

---

These commitments — five patient-state principles, five institutional-state principles, three interaction-semantics principles, five temporal-evolution principles — are the load-bearing claims of this note. If any one of the patient principles is wrong, the patient substrate fails. If any of the institutional principles is wrong, the institutional substrate fails. If the interaction semantics are wrong, the two-layer architecture fails as an integrated system even when each substrate is individually sound. If the temporal-evolution principles are wrong, the architecture eventually drifts into silent harm regardless of how well-engineered the other components are at deployment time. Disagreement on any of them is the most useful response this note can receive.

## 5. Where this sits in the architecture family

The substrate-first architecture branches the transformer family at the model-component level. *[inferred]* The most plausible learned component, given current technical maturity, is a recurrent transformer of the LDT type: a small attention stack unrolled for several iterations, projecting to and from a clinical-state lattice between forward passes. This is a transformer; it inherits the transformer's scaling properties, training methods, and engineering practices.

At the system level, the architecture is not a transformer. It is two coupled lattices — a patient-state lattice that handles per-patient reasoning, and an institutional-state lattice that handles cross-patient allocation — coupled across a third temporal axis where every substrate component carries explicit version metadata and currency status (Section 4D). *[inferred]* Each lattice has its own learned components (the patient lattice typically a recurrent transformer of the LDT type; the institutional lattice typically a mix of forecasting models, queue dynamics estimators, and operations-research solvers). Each lattice has its own deduction operators, its own provenance, its own abstention semantics. The lattices communicate through a defined event interface. Neither is subordinate to the other; both are necessary for the system to produce recommendations that are both sound and actionable. The system's identity is the coupled pair under the temporal-evolution axis, not either lattice in isolation.

This model-vs-system identity distinction matters for three audiences:

For researchers, the system is a hybrid neuro-symbolic architecture; the contribution is in the integration, not in any single component.

For deployment, the substrate is the product clinicians interact with; the model is an implementation detail constrained by the substrate.

For regulators, the substrate-first architecture maps onto the regulatory taxonomy proposed by Tan, Visva Gunasekeran et al. (npj Digital Medicine, Feb 2026). *[verified]* That taxonomy distinguishes three tiers of clinical software: Deterministic Clinical Software (DCS) with fixed input-data-output-label relationships and well-established SaMD pathways; Confined Clinical Software (CCS) using deep learning with a confined output spectrum and predictable variability, evaluable under existing FDA-SaMD guidelines; and Unconfined Non-Deterministic Clinical Software (UNDCS) using general-purpose LLMs operating across an open-ended semantic space with non-determinism arising from temperature sampling and floating-point inaccuracy. Tan et al. argue that current regulations are label-driven and break down for direct-to-consumer UNDCS, which technology providers distribute outside SaMD pathways with general-purpose disclaimers, and they call for a new regulatory paradigm that is not tied to manufacturer labeling. Their proposed mitigations include red teaming, guardrails, agent-agent moderation, and confined retrieval-augmented generation; they explicitly note that "neuro-symbolic models that reason deterministically from validated guidelines for improved reliability" are part of the path forward.

The substrate-first architecture is one specific answer to that call. *[inferred]* When LLM-class learned components are placed under the constraints Section 4 specifies — outputs filtered through the lattice's permissible-element gate, deduction operators applied to those filtered outputs, abstention emitted as a first-class output when soundness fails — the composite system's behavior is no longer UNDCS. The output spectrum is constrained by the lattice; the variability is bounded; the safety properties are architectural rather than learned. By Tan et al.'s definitions, the composite is CCS-class — evaluable under existing FDA-SaMD guidelines. The substrate-first architecture is a stronger version of their neuro-symbolic mitigation: the deterministic reasoning is enforced at the architectural seam between learned component and output, rather than as a behavioral preference that may degrade under distribution shift. This reframing matters because it offers a concrete regulatory path: a clinical AI system can use LLM-class components and remain regulable under existing SaMD frameworks, provided its substrate forces it into the CCS tier.

The two-layer extension (Section 4B) sits less cleanly within this taxonomy. *[inferred]* The patient substrate is CCS-class under the argument above. The institutional substrate, which mixes forecasting models with operations-research solvers, has different regulatory exposure: parts may fall under existing healthcare IT regulations rather than SaMD, and the interaction-semantics layer between the two lattices may not fit any current category. Section 8 names this two-layer regulatory complexity as a real cost of the architecture.

## 6. Prior art and the unclaimed synthesis

Each of the eighteen principles named above (five patient-state in 4A, five institutional-state in 4B, three interaction-semantics in 4C, five temporal-evolution in 4D) has independent support in published work between September 2025 and May 2026. The relevant references, sorted by which principle cluster they most directly address:

**Substrate philosophy and architectural separation of concerns.** *MedBeads: An Agent-Native, Immutable Data Substrate for Trustworthy Medical AI* (Nakajima, arXiv:2602.01086, Feb 2026). Proposes a Merkle DAG of cryptographically-linked clinical events as the substrate AI agents consume. Explicitly states the separation of concerns: the substrate guarantees what context the AI receives; the AI determines how to interpret it. Substrate is data-integrity-focused, not reasoning-soundness-focused.

**Neuro-symbolic clinical reasoning with ontology grounding.** Chavda, Suresh, Kumar, et al., *A bidirectional neuro-symbolic framework for clinical decision support via dynamic integration of deep learning and symbolic reasoning*, Network Modeling Analysis in Health Informatics and Bioinformatics, March 2026. Uses SNOMED CT and ICD-11 as ontology grounding. Uses knowledge graphs, not lattices with sound deduction operators. Does not claim architectural soundness.

**Abstention as a clinical AI requirement.** *KnowGuard: Knowledge-Driven Abstention for Multi-Round Clinical Reasoning* (Dang, Chen et al., arXiv:2509.24816, Sep 2025). Names the abstention requirement explicitly. Treats abstention as a behavior to train toward. Improves diagnostic accuracy by 3.93% and reduces unnecessary interaction by 7.27 turns. Behavioral, not architectural.

**Empirical evidence that scaling does not solve abstention.** Machcha, Yerra, et al., *Knowing When to Abstain: Medical LLMs Under Clinical Uncertainty* (introducing the MedAbstain benchmark), arXiv:2601.12471, Jan 2026. Benchmark combining conformal prediction, adversarial perturbations, and explicit abstention options. Finding: explicit abstention options improve safer abstention more than scaling or prompting. This is the strongest available empirical argument that the architecture, not the model size, is the binding constraint.

**State-abstraction with abstention semantics.** Tanwisuth & Leja, *Uncertainty-Aware Policy-Preserving Abstractions with Abstention for One-Shot Decisions*, MATS Research, Apr 2026. Proposes RL state abstractions that carry decision-margin information, triggering abstention when margins are small. Closest available formulation of "make abstraction carry abstention semantics." Reinforcement learning setting; not clinical.

**Auditable provenance in clinical neuro-symbolic systems.** Prenosil et al., *Neuro-symbolic AI for auditable cognitive information extraction from medical reports* (introducing the RUDS system), Communications Medicine (Nature portfolio), Nov 2025. Provides full back-tracing from final label to originating LLM tokens or human prompt. Applied to information extraction, not reasoning architectures.

**Sound abstract transformers as a technical building block.** Gu, Singh, Singh, *Cost-Driven Synthesis of Sound Abstract Interpreters*, arXiv:2511.13663, Nov 2025. Uses LLMs to synthesize sound abstract transformers for program analysis. The technical machinery substrate-first clinical AI would need for its deduction operators exists and works. Has not been pointed at clinical reasoning.

**The architectural-ceiling diagnosis.** Ingram & Merritt, *A Neural Affinity Framework for Abstract Reasoning*, Dec 2025. Independent evidence that transformer performance is bounded by architectural suitability, not curriculum or scale, on reasoning tasks.

**A neuro-symbolic three-module architecture for AI safety.** Jahn, Muskalla, Dargasz, Schramowski, Baum (DFKI, CERTAIN, Saarland Informatics Campus, TU Darmstadt, Hessian.AI), *Breaking Up with Normatively Monolithic Agency with GRACE: A Reason-Based Neuro-Symbolic Architecture for Safe and Ethical AI Alignment*, arXiv:2601.10520 (v2, Feb 2026). *[verified]* The structurally closest prior art to substrate-first clinical AI identified in this note's verification work. GRACE decomposes monolithic agents into three specialized modules: a Moral Module (MM) that applies a reason theory ⟨ℛ, 𝒟, <⟩ over Horty's defeasible deontic logic to derive a set Φ_perm of permissible Macro Action Types; a Decision-Making Module (DMM) that selects instrumentally optimal primitive actions within those constraints; and a Guard that enforces a ⊧_s φ for some permissible φ. The Guard's action space is explicitly 𝒜? — empty return represents abstention via rejection. The architecture is positioned as a contribution to the Guaranteed Safe AI agenda (Dalrymple et al. 2024) and demonstrated on a TherapAI example across five worked scenarios in Appendix B. An open-source implementation is currently in development. The Guard component is structurally equivalent to the substrate-enforced safety property proposed in this note, and the Guard's possible collapse into shielding (Alshiekh et al. 2018) maps cleanly onto the sound-deduction-via-lattice principle of substrate-first patient reasoning.

Two important narrowings of this note's originality claim follow from a careful reading of GRACE. First, GRACE is framed for general AI safety with clinical demonstrations, while substrate-first is clinical-specific with the lattice as a concrete commitment for clinical-state representation; the originality at the patient-substrate level is in the lattice formalism and the clinical-ontology integration, not in the modular-separation principle itself. Second, GRACE's footnote 1 states that "the framework is domain-agnostic: because normative reasons apply across normative domains, it can likewise address legal compliance, cultural conventions, organizational policies, and institutional requirements." *[verified]* This means the institutional substrate of Section 4B — which this note characterizes as a distinct architectural commitment — could be characterized instead as a specific GRACE instantiation applied to the institutional-norms domain, with capacity constraints and allocation policies as the relevant defaults. The independent contribution of Section 4B is then narrower than v0.6.0 framed: it is the coupling of two GRACE-like substrates through an explicit event interface (Section 4C), with joint licensing and joint abstention as cross-layer semantics — not the institutional-substrate architecture in isolation.

**Agentic paradigms for clinical communication.** Zhi, Zhao, Wu, Zhao (Tianjin University), Zhu (Chinese Academy of Sciences), *Reinventing Clinical Dialogue: Agentic Paradigms for LLM-Enabled Healthcare Communication*, arXiv:2512.01453, Dec 2025. *[verified]* First-principles survey of 300+ papers from venues including ACL, NeurIPS, ICLR, ICML, Nature Medicine, Nature Machine Intelligence, and npj Digital Medicine. Formalizes clinical dialogue as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process ⟨𝒮, 𝒜, 𝒪, 𝒯, 𝒵, R, γ⟩ with belief state b_t maintained via Bayesian update over the latent pathology Φ ⊂ s_t. The agentic paradigm is operationalized through five orthogonal components: strategic planning (generating a plan 𝒫_t as constraint on the policy space), memory management (a transition function S_t = μ(S_{t-1}, o_t, a_{t-1}, 𝒦) approximating Bayesian belief update), action execution, multi-agent collaboration over a topology graph, and self-evolution. The survey's four-paradigm taxonomy is organized along orthogonal axes — Knowledge Source (Implicit ↔ Explicit) × Agency Objective (Event Cognition ↔ Goal Execution) — yielding Latent Space Clinician (Med-PaLM, MedAgents), Emergent Planner (AgentMD with ReAct), Grounded Synthesizer (Med-RAG, MA-COIR), and Verifiable Workflow Automator paradigms; the authors classify AMIE within Verifiable Workflow Automator. The paper explicitly identifies neuro-symbolic integration as a critical open frontier for future research. The survey is conceptual-landscape work that catalogs approaches but does not propose a specific architectural implementation. Substrate-first clinical AI sits within the Verifiable Workflow Automator category in this taxonomy but commits to stronger architectural guarantees than the average VWA implementation surveyed: substrate-enforced abstention rather than workflow-constrained outputs, sound deduction operators rather than retrieval-grounded synthesis, lattice-typed partial-information state rather than POMDP belief approximation via memory transition.

**Modeling framework for ethical clinical AI agents.** Chow (Princess Margaret Cancer Centre, UofT Radiation Medicine) and Li (UofT English Dept), *From Dialogue Systems to Autonomous Agents: A Modeling Framework for Ethical Generative AI in Healthcare*, MDPI Information 17(4):361, April 2026. *[verified]* Conceptual review proposing a modeling framework organized along seven interrelated dimensions: autonomy gradients, state persistence, tool orchestration, personalization, workflow coupling, human-AI co-agency, and governance integration. Names three risk dimensions — epistemic (knowledge representation), structural (workflow embedding and inter-component dependencies), and normative (accountability and fairness). Argues that ethical risks emerge from longitudinal system interactions rather than isolated outputs, and that fairness constraints, transparency mechanisms, and lifecycle governance should be embedded directly within AI design rather than added as post-hoc safeguards. Worked example is a radiotherapy treatment planning agent. The paper is conceptual-framing rather than architectural specification: the autonomy-gradient and seven-dimension framework offers a vocabulary for analyzing agentic clinical AI, not an engineering proposal. The framing is heavily anchored to Geoffrey Hinton's recent statements (Massey College talk, University of Toronto) about agentic AI risks, and the architectural recommendations remain at the level of "embed governance as a structural component" without specifying how. Useful prior art for the autonomy-gradient and lifecycle-governance framing language; lighter contribution to the integration question than GRACE or Reinventing Clinical Dialogue. Substrate-first principles are compatible with the seven-dimension framework — the lattice and event interface offer concrete mechanisms for state persistence, tool orchestration, and workflow coupling — without being a specialization of it.

**Regulation of agentic clinical AI.** Tan, Visva Gunasekeran (co-equal first authors), Low, Sim, Foo, Morris, Wong (Yong Loo Lin School of Medicine NUS, Singapore National Eye Centre, Duke-NUS, Singapore MOH, Tsinghua Medicine), *Regulation of clinical Artificial Intelligence (AI) in the Age of Agents: Unconfined Non-Deterministic Clinical Software (UNDCS) systems for healthcare*, npj Digital Medicine 9:186, Feb 2026, CC BY-NC-ND 4.0. *[verified]* A Matters Arising response to Weissman et al. (npj Digital Medicine 8:148, 2025). Introduces a three-tier regulatory taxonomy: Deterministic Clinical Software (DCS, fixed input-data-output-label relationships, well-handled by existing SaMD pathways), Confined Clinical Software (CCS, deep learning with confined output spectrum, predictable variability, evaluable under expanded datasets via existing FDA-SaMD guidelines), and Unconfined Non-Deterministic Clinical Software (UNDCS, general-purpose LLM-based, open-ended semantic space, non-determinism arising from temperature sampling and floating-point inaccuracy). Argues current regulations are label-driven and break down for direct-to-consumer UNDCS distributed outside SaMD pathways. Proposes red teaming, guardrails, agent-agent moderation in multi-agent system architectures, and confined RAG as mitigation strategies, and explicitly notes that these can be augmented by "neuro-symbolic models that reason deterministically from validated guidelines for improved reliability." Calls for a new regulatory paradigm not tied to manufacturer labeling. Section 5 now engages substantively with this taxonomy: the substrate-first architecture is positioned as one specific architectural answer to Tan et al.'s call, converting UNDCS-class systems with LLM-class components into CCS-class composites evaluable under existing SaMD frameworks.

**Production deterministic policy enforcement for agents.** Microsoft (Imran Siddique, Principal Group Engineering Manager), *Agent Governance Toolkit*, April 2026, open-source MIT-licensed. *[verified]* The first toolkit to address all ten OWASP Agentic AI Top 10 risks (Dec 2025) via deterministic, sub-millisecond policy enforcement (<0.1ms p99). Seven independently installable packages across Python, TypeScript, Rust, Go, and .NET: Agent OS (stateless policy engine — explicitly described as "the kernel for AI agents," supporting YAML rules, OPA Rego, and Cedar policy languages), Agent Mesh (DID-based cryptographic identity with Ed25519, dynamic trust scoring), Agent Runtime (execution rings, saga orchestration, kill switch), Agent SRE (SLOs, error budgets, circuit breakers), Agent Compliance (regulatory framework mapping to EU AI Act, HIPAA, SOC2), Agent Marketplace (plugin signing and supply-chain security), and Agent Lightning (RL training governance). The OWASP Top 10 mapping is explicit: semantic intent classifier for goal hijacking, capability sandboxing for tool misuse, Cross-Model Verification Kernel with majority voting for memory poisoning, ring isolation plus trust decay plus automated kill switch for rogue agents. Framework-agnostic adapters exist for LangChain, CrewAI, Google ADK, Microsoft Agent Framework, Dify, LlamaIndex, OpenAI Agents SDK, Haystack, LangGraph, PydanticAI. Architectural motivation is OS-inspired: "We asked: what if we took these proven, battle-tested patterns — kernels, privilege rings, process isolation; mTLS and identity; SLOs and circuit breakers — and applied them to AI agents?" Key methodological commitment: "Make security the default, not an add-on. We built governance into the execution path, intercepting actions, rather than as an optional wrapper. Optional security tends to go unadopted." Regulatory drivers are EU AI Act high-risk obligations (August 2026) and Colorado AI Act (June 2026). Not clinical-specific, but the architectural pattern — runtime deterministic constraints enforced at the seam between agent and action, framed via OS-kernel analogy — is implementation precedent for the substrate-enforcement principle. The toolkit's framing of governance-as-default-not-add-on is the production-engineering version of the architectural-vs-behavioral distinction this note's Section 4 draws.

**Capacity-aware allocation and decision infrastructure.** The literature in operations research, health services research, and healthcare AI on clinical resource allocation under capacity constraints — previously flagged *[bounded]* and queued for survey — is now engaged with at the cited level. Six pieces of prior art are most directly relevant to Section 4B's institutional substrate. A residual *[bounded]* scope remains: the broader operations research community's work on robust optimization, the health-services-research literature on allocation equity beyond the entries below, the queueing-theory literature applied to emergency and intensive care at depth, and the mass-casualty and pandemic-triage literature have not been surveyed exhaustively.

*Allocation-aware healthcare AI.* Ferzana, *Beyond Accuracy: A Decision-Theoretic Framework for Allocation-Aware Healthcare AI*, arXiv:2601.06161, Jan 2026 (AIiH 2026 Imperial College London). *[cited]* Names the "allocation gap" — the empirical disconnect between predictive accuracy and patient outcomes. Formalizes healthcare delivery as a scarcity-constrained stochastic allocation problem and positions AI as "decision infrastructure that estimates utility rather than making autonomous decisions," using constrained optimization and Markov decision processes to analyze how improved estimation alters optimal allocation under binding capacity. Synthetic triage simulation shows allocation-aware policies outperform conventional risk-threshold policies by 18–25% in realized utility at identical predictive accuracy. This is the closest existing framing to Section 4B's institutional substrate: the "AI as decision infrastructure under constraints" formulation is conceptually almost identical to what Section 4B proposes, applied to the allocation question only. The two-layer originality claim in this note narrows accordingly: Ferzana's framework is the institutional layer of substrate-first reasoning, published; Section 4B's residual originality is the explicit coupling with a patient-state lattice through joint licensing and joint abstention semantics (Section 4C).

*Capacity-constrained MDPs with parameter uncertainty for medical allocation.* Demiray, Güneş, Örmeci (Koç University), *Modeling and Optimizing Resource Allocation Decisions through Multi-model Markov Decision Processes with Capacity Constraints*, arXiv:2011.04357. *[cited]* Capacity-constrained, finite-horizon MDP with uncertain transition probabilities and rewards, motivated by patients with multiple chronic diseases facing limited special-care capacity. Notes prior work by Ayvaci et al. and Cevik et al. using constrained MDP and POMDP for breast cancer screening decisions. Operations-research methodology applied to medical allocation with parameter uncertainty as the methodological contribution.

*Decision-aware learning for health supply chains.* Chung, Rostami, H. Bastani, O. Bastani (U Penn, Macro-Eyes), *Decision-Aware Learning for Optimizing Health Supply Chains*, arXiv:2211.08507. *[cited]* Combines machine learning for demand prediction with optimization for allocation, explicitly naming the alignment problem between the ML loss function and the downstream decision loss. Being deployed with the Sierra Leone National Medical Supplies Agency. Real-world allocation infrastructure in a low-income setting, directly relevant to Section 1's institutional-blindness failure mode and to the institutional substrate's deployment in LMIC contexts where the allocation problem is the dominant constraint.

*Measurized MDPs for population health management.* Adelman, Olivares-Nadal, Xie, *Optimizing Treatment Allocation to Maximize the Health of a Population*, arXiv:2604.07738, April 2026. *[cited]* Lifts traditional MDP to the space of probability measures via the measurized framework (Adelman and Olivares-Nadal, 2026a), with timewise dualization of the capacity constraint using time-dependent Lagrange multipliers. *Equity-aware geospatial AI for hospital allocation.* *Equity-Aware Geospatial AI for Forecasting Demand-Driven Hospital Locations in Germany*, arXiv:2510.10640, October 2025. *[cited]* Agentic AI planner formulating hospital location optimization as a mixed-integer linear program, integrating ARIMA demand forecasting, equity index, accessibility score, and hospital facility demand ratio. Not clinical decision support but a spatial-allocation pipeline that uses conceptual vocabulary adjacent to Section 4B (agentic AI planner, integrated demand-equity-optimization pipeline).

*Equity at the point of care.* *Equity at the point of care: auditing AI-supported resource allocation in obstetric emergencies*, Frontiers in Public Health, doi:10.3389/fpubh.2026.1774367, February 2026. *[cited]* Introduces the MFAS framework for measuring equity in obstetric emergency response. Observes that comparable clinical risk does not consistently translate into comparable speed or intensity of response because of workflow design, triage thresholds, paging cascades, transport logistics, and competing cases — a direct empirical statement of Section 1's institutional-blindness failure mode in a specific clinical domain.

**The constructive proof of the sound-deduction-via-lattice principle.** Davis, Haller, Alfarano, Santolucito, *Lattice Deduction Transformers*, arXiv:2605.08605, May 2026. Demonstrates that a small recurrent transformer with an externalized lattice can achieve 100% accuracy and empirical soundness on benchmarks where frontier LLMs score 0%. Validates the technical approach on closed-world combinatorial puzzles. Authors note that ARC-like tasks require a different abstract domain — one over the programs that produce solutions — which is exactly the open problem clinical extension would face.

**Visible commercial and research approaches that do not yet implement substrate-first principles.** Two examples warrant explicit naming because they are widely cited as the frontier of clinical AI and could be mistakenly characterized as substrate-first.

*Glass Health* (Paul, Ramsey, 2021–present). Combines a base LLM with a proprietary clinical knowledge database and physician reinforcement training. Founder characterization to TechCrunch (Sep 2023): "Our LLM application retrieves physician-validated clinical guidelines as AI context at the time it generates outputs." *[cited]* Independent characterization confirms the architecture as Retrieval-Augmented Generation with physician-validated grounding. This is sophisticated clinical RAG: the LLM does the reasoning, the knowledge graph provides cited sources, the grounding is high-quality. It is adjacent to substrate-first in framing but not in architecture. There is no published evidence that the LLM is architecturally prevented from generating contraindicated recommendations; the safety property is behavioral, conditioned on retrieval quality and reinforcement training, not architectural.

*AMIE* (Google Research / DeepMind). The original AMIE paper (Tu et al., arXiv:2401.05654, Jan 2024) introduces an inference-time chain-of-reasoning strategy. The newer disease-management variant (arXiv:2503.06074, March 2026) leverages Gemini's long-context capabilities, combining in-context retrieval with structured reasoning over multiple patient visits. *[cited]* The belief state is held in the context window via long-context attention and retrieval, not in architectural state. AMIE represents the frontier of what chain-of-reasoning plus long-context retrieval can achieve in clinical dialogue. It is exactly the failure mode this note critiques — partial-information state held in prompt rather than enforced architecturally — implemented sophisticatedly at the frontier of current technique.

The verification work for v0.6.1 examined the actual published architectures and reached the opposite conclusion. Both systems represent serious clinical AI engineering. Neither implements substrate-enforced safety in the sense Section 4 specifies.

**Temporal-evolution standards and regulatory infrastructure.** Section 4D's principles for handling rolling evolution of clinical evidence, guidelines, and policies do not start from scratch; they integrate with four mature standards and regulatory frameworks that already address adjacent portions of the problem.

*HL7 FHIR Clinical Reasoning Module and Clinical Quality Language.* The FHIR Clinical Reasoning Module provides PlanDefinition, ActivityDefinition, Library, and Measure resources with explicit version, effectivePeriod, and status metadata for computable clinical knowledge artifacts. *[verified]* Clinical Quality Language (CQL) is the expression language for the underlying logic, with CQL 1.5.3 published as an ANSI Normative Standard and CQL 2.0.0 in trial-use ballot. Computable clinical guidelines is a stated use case. FHIR CR plus CQL is the standards-track substrate-component versioning infrastructure that Section 4D.1's versioning principle integrates with.

*Cochrane Living Systematic Reviews, MAGICapp, Australian Living Evidence Collaboration.* The living systematic review methodology, defined formally by Cochrane in 2019 guidance after initial proposal in 2014, replaces periodic SR updates with continuous monitoring and rapid incorporation of new evidence. *[verified]* MAGICapp is the web-based collaborative platform for publishing living guidelines using GRADE methodology. The Australian Living Evidence Collaboration coordinates living-guideline production across multiple clinical domains. Three criteria identify topics suitable for LSR treatment: priority for decision-making, low certainty in existing evidence, and likely new research evidence. This is the evidence-currency infrastructure that Section 4D.2's currency-as-signal principle consumes.

*FDA Predetermined Change Control Plans.* The FDA published final guidance on PCCPs for AI-enabled device software functions on December 4, 2024 ("Marketing Submission Recommendations for a Predetermined Change Control Plan for Artificial Intelligence-Enabled Device Software Functions"), with statutory authority under FD&C Act Section 515C. *[verified]* PCCPs allow manufacturers to pre-authorize device modifications without requiring new marketing submissions for each change, particularly relevant for AI/ML systems designed to evolve. The August 2025 joint FDA, Health Canada, and UK MHRA five guiding principles for ML-enabled medical device PCCPs (Focused, Risk-based, Evidence-based, Transparent, Lifecycle-oriented) provide harmonized regulatory expectations. PCCP framework is the regulatory envelope Section 4D.3's sound-evolution principle operates within for learned components.

*EU AI Act Article 72 post-market monitoring.* The EU AI Act requires providers of high-risk AI systems to establish a post-market monitoring system that actively collects and reviews data on performance throughout the system's lifetime. *[verified]* The post-market monitoring plan is part of Annex IV technical documentation. The Commission was required to adopt an implementing act with the plan template by February 2, 2026. Outputs feed into Article 9 risk management updates and Article 73 serious incident reporting. Clinical AI systems regulated as high-risk under Annex III fall directly under Article 72; the regulatory expectation that AI systems are monitored across their deployment lifetime is now in force law in the EU and Section 4D.5's evolution-aware provenance principle is the architectural answer to this regulatory requirement.

These four are mature, normative, and individually adopted in production. The integration of all four into a single deployed clinical substrate is the engineering work that does not yet exist at scale; Section 8 names this as a current limitation. The 4D principles are bets on what that integration should look like, anchored in standards and regulatory mechanisms that already exist.

## 7. What would have to be built

The substrate-first architecture is not a startup thesis as of May 2026. It is a research program. The build splits into three core construction parts — a patient-state substrate (7A), an institutional-state substrate (7B), and the integration infrastructure between them (7C) — plus a fourth concern: temporal-evolution integration with the standards and regulatory frameworks named in Section 4D (HL7 FHIR Clinical Reasoning, Cochrane living systematic reviews and MAGICapp, FDA Predetermined Change Control Plans, EU AI Act Article 72). The fourth concern is engineering integration rather than architectural design from scratch, because those standards already exist and the substrate's job is to consume their outputs and align with their requirements; the engineering scope of that integration is named in Section 8 as a current limitation and is part of the 2–4 year deployment horizon Section 7's timeline predicts.

### 7A. Patient-state substrate

**A clinical lattice specification.** Formal description of candidate sets — diagnoses, medications, findings, states — with orderings, joins, meets, ⊥ conditions, and the meaning of each. Drawn from SNOMED CT, RxNorm, LOINC, ICD-11, but lifted to lattice structure with explicit operations rather than treated as flat reference data.

**A deduction operator library.** Symbolic rules that act soundly on the lattice. Some hand-specified from clinical guidelines — Sepsis-3, KDIGO, Wells, CURB-65, qSOFA, validated risk scores. Some learned under soundness constraints. Each operator declares its input conditions, its output transformation, and the evidence required to license it.

**A learned refinement model.** The LDT-style component. Trained to propose which deduction operators to apply next, which branches to explore, which candidates to prioritize. Constrained by the substrate to never violate soundness.

**A search and backtrack procedure.** The symbolic outer loop. Handles cases where deduction stalls. Manages parallel hypotheses. Triggers abstention when search exhausts without commitment. This is where conflict-driven clause learning, in some form, becomes clinically meaningful: the system learns from failed branches by recording which combinations are guaranteed to fail.

### 7B. Institutional-state substrate

**An institutional state specification.** Formal description of resource state — bed counts by acuity level, OR and infusion-chair schedules, pharmacy inventory by drug and lot, laboratory and microbiology cycle times, on-call coverage by specialty, blood product stock, dialysis and ventilator capacity. The lattice structure here is different from the patient lattice: resource state is fundamentally quantitative and temporal rather than candidate-set elimination. The lattice elements are resource configurations; the partial order is "feasibility under capacity."

**A capacity-update operator library.** Symbolic rules that act soundly on the institutional state. Admission allocates a bed; discharge releases one. Pharmacy dispensing decrements inventory with provenance to a prescription; delivery increments it with provenance to a procurement order. Cancellation of an OR slot releases capacity to the queue under named priority rules. Each operator declares its trigger, its state transformation, and the authority under which it is invoked.

**Capacity-learned components.** Demand forecasting (anticipated admissions over the next 6, 24, 72 hours), queue dynamics prediction (expected wait times under current trajectory), length-of-stay estimation, supply depletion projection. These are constrained by the institutional substrate to never propose impossible allocations — they predict, the substrate licenses.

**An allocation policy layer.** The institutional analogue of search-and-backtrack. Handles cases where the patient queue exceeds capacity and explicit priority rules must be invoked: clinical acuity, time waited, equity criteria, ethical frameworks for scarcity allocation. The policy layer is hand-specified, not learned — it encodes the institution's allocation ethics as auditable rules rather than as learned approximations.

### 7C. Integration infrastructure

**A defined cross-layer event interface.** Patient-lattice updates emit typed events to the institutional lattice; institutional-lattice updates emit typed events back. Events carry provenance: which patient state caused this resource request, which capacity change triggered this re-prioritization. The interface is the formal contract between the substrates.

**A clinician interface that exposes both layers.** Clinicians see the patient candidate set with eliminations and provenance (the patient layer), AND the institutional state that affects what the system can commit to (the institutional layer), AND the cross-patient prioritization the system is computing (the interaction). The unconstrained-optimal action and the institutionally-actionable plan appear together with explicit diff. Clinician overrides happen at the appropriate layer and are logged as first-class operations.

**A capacity-management interface.** The institutional layer is not invisible to administrators. Capacity managers see the current institutional state, the active demand forecasts, the pending allocation events, and the queues. They can invoke capacity-update operators directly — open a surge bed, expedite a supply procurement, reallocate a queue — and these actions are logged with named authority.

**A joint evaluation harness.** Patient-level metrics (soundness rate, calibrated abstention rate, provenance completeness, clinician override behavior) are necessary but not sufficient. Add: capacity-respecting recommendation rate (what fraction of recommendations are actually executable given current institutional state), queue fairness (does the system produce equitable wait times across patient populations), resource utilization efficiency, allocation override behavior, and joint-abstention quality. The harness measures the integrated system, not the substrates in isolation.

### 7D. First narrow applications, re-examined under the patient/institutional pairing

The applications listed in earlier versions of this note are the same; the patient/institutional pairing changes how they should be approached. Each has both a patient dimension and an institutional dimension, and the institutional dimension is often where the architectural value is most legible. The temporal-evolution axis (Section 4D) applies systemically to each application but is treated under 4D and exemplified in 7E.6 rather than enumerated per-application here, because the temporal concerns are about each application's deduction-operator currency rather than about how each application's patient or institutional state evolves at a single point in time.

*Antimicrobial stewardship.* Patient: which antibiotic is appropriate given suspected pathogen, allergy history, renal function, prior cultures. Institutional: which antibiotics are currently in stock and for how many days, what the local antibiogram looks like, what the stewardship committee's restriction rules are, what the cost-coverage policy permits. Both dimensions are codifiable; the two-layer architecture produces recommendations that are clinically appropriate AND currently dispensable.

*Transfusion compatibility and inventory.* Patient: which blood products are safe for this patient given prior reactions, current antibody screen, clinical indication. Institutional: what is in the blood bank right now, expiration dates, donor pool projections, cross-match capacity. The safety asymmetry is extreme on both sides — wrong-product transfusion is fatal, no-product-available delays are also fatal.

*Anticoagulation management.* Largely patient-level for the reasoning, but institutional state matters for INR-monitoring lab capacity, anticoagulant supply, and inter-facility transfer when reversal agents are required and not locally available.

*Chemotherapy dose verification.* Patient: dose calculation against current weight, renal/hepatic function, prior toxicity, regimen-specific dose modifications. Institutional: drug supply, infusion chair scheduling, nurse-to-patient ratios for high-acuity infusions, pharmacy compounding capacity.

*Drug-drug interaction reasoning.* Mostly patient-level by default, but institutional substitution availability matters when the safe alternative is not in formulary or is on backorder.

These five are the deployment candidates. Section 7E that follows takes a different tack: rather than enumerating where to deploy, it sketches how the architecture instantiates across diverse clinical decisions, including one decision where the architecture is the wrong choice.

### 7E. Sketched worked examples and an anti-example

Section 4's principles are domain-general claims. They are tested not by the depth of any single instantiation but by the diversity of instantiations that share the same architectural commitments. Four sketched worked examples follow — Sepsis-3 prognostic stratification, KDIGO AKI staging, Wells score for pulmonary embolism, and CURB-65 for community-acquired pneumonia disposition — at sketch-depth rather than full specification. Each illustrates a different aspect of the architecture: Sepsis-3 stresses temporal refinement and lab-result-latency, KDIGO stresses contraindication enforcement across organ systems, Wells stresses discrete-feature scoring with sequential-test refinement, and CURB-65 stresses joint patient-institutional disposition reasoning. The depth at which any one example is specified is bounded deliberately; the architecture's claim is the through-line that should be visible across the four, not the domain-specific machinery that would accumulate under full specification of any single one. A fifth example — chronic depression management — is included at limitation-depth as an anti-example, illustrating a clinical domain where the architecture's assumptions are stressed enough that hybrid or alternative approaches are likely more appropriate. The anti-example is part of the scope-claim, not a refutation of it: the architecture's value lies in being precise about where it applies and where it does not. A sixth example (7E.6, added in v0.10.0) takes a different approach from the first five: rather than demonstrating the architecture instantiated at a single point in time across diverse domains, it demonstrates the architecture under temporal evolution by walking through the live Surviving Sepsis Campaign 2021 → 2026 guideline transition that occurred in March 2026, exercising Section 4D's five principles against a real recent transition rather than against a hypothetical one. 7E.6 is to 4D what 7E.1 through 7E.4 are to 4A and 4B: a worked example that gives the principles a concrete instantiation rather than leaving them as abstract commitments.

#### 7E.1 Sepsis-3 prognostic stratification in a capacity-constrained tertiary setting

A patient-level model predicts 28-day mortality risk at T0 from routine hematological parameters — extended inflammatory parameters, immature platelet fraction, immature granulocytes, with clinical covariates — and produces a calibrated probability and a three-tier risk stratification. The patient lattice carries the Sepsis-3 candidate set {no sepsis, sepsis, septic shock} refined by SOFA component operators (respiratory PaO₂/FiO₂, coagulation platelet count, liver bilirubin, cardiovascular MAP and vasopressor requirement, CNS GCS, renal creatinine and urine output) and the qSOFA bedside screening operator. Under the patient-only architecture, the output is the risk tier with attribution explanations. Under the two-layer architecture, the output additionally specifies: current ICU bed availability for the patient's acuity tier; the institution's preferred broad-spectrum antibiotic protocol availability and remaining supply duration; the microbiology lab cycle time relative to the patient's expected window for empiric therapy decisions; the on-call intensivist's current load. For a high-tier risk patient, the system produces an unconstrained-optimal plan (immediate ICU admission, preferred broad-spectrum protocol, hourly monitoring) AND an institutionally-actionable plan (HDU admission with explicit escalation triggers, available-supply alternative regimen, deferred culture processing with empiric coverage rationale logged). The diff is visible to the clinician. The institutional substrate emits an event to the bed queue for ICU placement when available. The patient substrate logs the constrained plan with provenance to the institutional state at the moment of decision. If the patient deteriorates and the substrate fires an escalation, the queue re-prioritization is itself a logged deduction step with named authority. What this example illustrates: temporal refinement (sepsis status evolves with each new lab and vital, requiring soundness preservation under update), the integration of a learned prognostic model under substrate constraints, and the lab-result-latency problem where empiric antibiotic decisions must be made before culture results return — addressed architecturally by emitting time-bounded provisional commitments with explicit re-evaluation events when new information arrives.

#### 7E.2 KDIGO AKI staging and contraindication enforcement

A patient develops a creatinine increase from a baseline of 0.9 to 1.4 mg/dL over 36 hours with urine output averaging 0.6 mL/kg/h. The patient lattice's AKI candidate set begins at {none, stage 1, stage 2, stage 3} and is refined to {stage 1} by a creatinine-trajectory operator that requires a baseline value as an input precondition. The urine-output-trajectory operator refines independently and must reconcile when the two paths disagree. The contraindication propagator then enforces: stage ≥1 requires renal dose adjustment for the patient's currently active drug list where applicable; stage ≥2 requires explicit risk-benefit documentation before IV iodinated contrast at standard volume (current evidence with low-osmolar contrast agents makes this a clinical-judgment decision rather than absolute prohibition, but the architecture's role is to surface the decision and log the justification, not to silently allow it); stage ≥3 contraindicates several common drug classes at standard dosing including NSAIDs and continued ACE-I/ARB without explicit indication, and requires nephrotoxic-drug review for currently active orders including aminoglycosides, vancomycin at standard troughs, and other agents with renal clearance. Abstention triggers fire when baseline creatinine is unknown (the architecture cannot soundly stage without it and emits a request for baseline establishment), when urine output gaps make the 6–12 hour window assessment unreliable, or when confounding factors (recent contrast administration, rhabdomyolysis suspicion, postrenal obstruction) make AKI versus pseudo-AKI distinction uncertain. The institutional substrate carries nephrology consult queue, RRT capacity by shift (machines, dialysis nurses, dialyzer availability), vascular-access placement availability for emergent RRT, and the pharmacy dose-adjustment service load. What this example illustrates: the contraindication-enforcement property of the architecture is the load-bearing safety claim, separated cleanly from the prognostic property. A patient may not be at high mortality risk yet still have ordered contraindications that the substrate must architecturally prevent, and the architecture's value here is in the prevention rather than in the prediction.

#### 7E.3 Wells score for pulmonary embolism with sequential testing

A patient presents with pleuritic chest pain and tachycardia. The PE candidate set begins at {excluded, unlikely-D-dimer-needed, likely-CTPA-needed, confirmed}. Wells component operators each refine the set discretely: clinical signs of DVT (+3), PE as most likely diagnosis (+3), heart rate >100 (+1.5), recent surgery or immobilization (+1.5), prior DVT/PE (+1.5), hemoptysis (+1), malignancy (+1). At a score threshold the category-commit operator commits to a category. The sequential-testing operator then refines further: D-dimer result requires the Wells category as prior, where low pre-test probability plus negative D-dimer yields PE excluded, while high pre-test probability requires CTPA regardless of D-dimer. The contraindication propagator handles the worst case: PE confirmed plus active major bleeding emits joint abstention from the standard anticoagulation recommendation and an institutional event for IR consultation regarding IVC filter placement. Abstention triggers fire when Wells gestalt is unclear (clinician judgment substitutes for one or more components and the architecture emits the component requests rather than a category), when D-dimer is unavailable within the clinical window, or when CTPA is contraindicated (severe renal impairment without acceptable contrast-protective protocol, anaphylaxis history without premedication option) and an alternative imaging path must be requested. The institutional substrate carries CTPA scanner queue and contrast availability, heparin nomogram service load, IR consult queue, and bed availability stratified by PE acuity. What this example illustrates: discrete-feature scoring with sequential-test refinement maps cleanly onto sound deduction operators with explicit input preconditions, and the contraindication-enforcement property produces architecturally-safe outputs even in worst-case patient configurations where the obvious recommendation is forbidden.

#### 7E.4 CURB-65 for community-acquired pneumonia disposition

A patient with confirmed CAP requires a disposition decision: outpatient, ward admission, or ICU admission. Per current 2019 IDSA/ATS guidelines, CURB-65 (or PSI) is used for the inpatient versus outpatient distinction, while IDSA/ATS major and minor criteria specifically determine ICU need. The disposition candidate set begins at {outpatient, ward, ICU}. The CURB-65 sub-operator refines the outpatient-vs-inpatient distinction using component operators: confusion (+1), urea >7 mmol/L or BUN >19 mg/dL (+1), respiratory rate ≥30 (+1), blood pressure with SBP <90 or DBP ≤60 (+1), age ≥65 (+1). Tier mapping: scores 0–1 map to outpatient appropriate, score 2 maps to ward admission with consideration of supervised outpatient care, scores 3–5 map to ward admission with separate ICU evaluation required. The IDSA/ATS criteria operator then determines ICU need independently of CURB-65 score: one major criterion (septic shock requiring vasopressors, or respiratory failure requiring mechanical ventilation) or three or more minor criteria (confusion, RR ≥30, PaO₂/FiO₂ <250, multilobar infiltrates, uremia, leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, hypothermia, hypotension requiring aggressive fluids) refines disposition to ICU. The empiric antibiotic operator selects from institutional guidance based on severity tier, prior antibiotic exposure, and local resistance patterns. Abstention triggers fire when confusion assessment is ambiguous (delirium versus baseline cognitive impairment requires formal AMS evaluation and the architecture emits the request), when urea is missing (CRB-65 fallback is permitted with explicitly narrower precision and the lattice tier remains a union over two adjacent tiers), or when the CURB-65 score and IDSA/ATS criteria disagree (high CURB-65 with no IDSA/ATS criteria, or low CURB-65 with rapidly evolving criteria — joint reasoning required, abstention emitted on the ICU question with continued ward recommendation pending re-evaluation). The institutional substrate carries bed availability stratified by tier, ICU acuity matching, empiric antibiotic protocol availability and supply, and chest imaging queue. What this example illustrates: the joint patient-institutional disposition decision is the architecture's natural home — the patient layer produces a recommended tier, the institutional layer constrains what dispositions are feasible, and the joint output is the actionable plan rather than the unconstrained recommendation that ignores institutional state.

#### 7E.5 Anti-example: chronic depression management

This example is included not because the architecture handles it well, but because it does not. *[inferred]* Substrate-first clinical AI is designed under assumptions that hold strongly in acute care and weaken substantially in chronic management of conditions where individual-level treatment-response prediction is poorly supported and patient preferences are load-bearing. Four assumptions are stressed in depression management.

First, lattice element crispness. PHQ-9 is quantitative, but the underlying diagnostic categories are heterogeneous. Major depressive disorder overlaps adjustment disorder, bereavement, bipolar depression in its depressed phase, anxiety with depressed mood, substance-related mood disturbance, and treatment-resistant depression — categories that share symptoms but differ in optimal management. ICD-11 and DSM-5 carve the diagnostic space at administrative joints rather than biological ones. The lattice's information ordering becomes weak: refining "depression" to "major depressive disorder" adds less actionable information than refining "sepsis" to "septic shock."

Second, deduction operator soundness. The deductive premise that a specific medication will benefit a specific patient is poorly supported at the individual level for most depression treatments. The STAR*D trial (Rush et al., 2006) reported a Level 1 (first-line SSRI) remission rate of 36.8% by QIDS-SR (approximately 28% by HRSD), with diminishing rates at each subsequent step: 30.6%, 13.7%, and 13.0% by QIDS-SR for Levels 2, 3, and 4. The original report's 67% cumulative remission claim has been contested by Pigott et al. (BMJ 2023), whose reanalysis of the patient-level data set suggests cumulative remission was closer to 35%. Whichever figure is accepted, the pattern is the same: first-line antidepressant monotherapy achieves remission in roughly one-third of patients, response (defined as ≥50% symptom reduction, a less clinically meaningful endpoint than remission) in approximately half, and each subsequent treatment step adds diminishing absolute benefit. The sound-deduction property the architecture relies on for safety — if the deduction operator licenses the recommendation, the recommendation is supported by the evidence base — becomes vacuous when the deduction's evidence is "this medication helps approximately a third of the population to remission at this stage of treatment, and we cannot predict which third in advance." Architectural soundness preservation is real; clinical informativeness of the individual recommendation is not.

Third, useful abstention degenerate. The honest answer to "will this specific patient benefit from sertraline" is "I cannot say with confidence." The architecture's abstention output, intended as a first-class safety property that distinguishes substrate-first from current-paradigm clinical AI, becomes the trivially correct answer for individual treatment-selection decisions. A system that abstains correctly on most depression treatment-selection decisions is correct but useless to the clinician who needs to choose; a system that does not abstain in these contexts violates its own soundness commitments. Neither failure mode is recoverable within the architecture as specified.

Fourth, institutional substrate thinness. Outpatient depression management is rarely capacity-constrained in the way acute care is. Psychiatrist availability matters for severe and complex cases; outpatient pharmacotherapy is largely capacity-unconstrained at the prescribing decision. The institutional layer, which carries substantial signal in acute care, carries little signal here. The architectural value of the two-layer framing is correspondingly small.

What this implies for the architecture's scope. *[inferred]* The substrate-first architecture is a strong fit for clinical domains where lattice elements are crisp, deduction operators have direct individual-level evidence, abstention is informative rather than trivially true, and the institutional substrate carries signal. Acute care fits these criteria across many domains, which is why the four sketched examples above are all from acute or near-acute territory. Chronic management of conditions with thin individual-level evidence and strong patient-preference content does not fit, and depression is a representative case. The architecture is not refuted by the depression example; its scope is bounded by it. A hybrid approach for depression management might use substrate-first only for the components that fit — drug-drug interaction checking, contraindication enforcement, suicide-risk assessment with explicit lattice elements, monitoring for serotonin syndrome or hyponatremia — while using alternative approaches for the treatment-selection components that do not, including shared decision-making frameworks, measurement-based care algorithms, and structured patient preference elicitation. The limitation is not a deficiency in the architecture; it is the architecture's honest scope.

#### 7E.6 Worked example: Surviving Sepsis Campaign 2021 → 2026 transition through the substrate

The four examples above demonstrate the architecture instantiated at a single point in time. This example demonstrates the architecture under temporal evolution, exercising Section 4D's principles against a real, recent guideline transition rather than a hypothetical one.

In March 2026, the Surviving Sepsis Campaign published its 2026 update, replacing the 2021 guidelines with 129 statements of which 46 were new (Prescott et al., *Crit Care Med*, March 23, 2026, doi:10.1097/CCM.0000000000007075). *[verified]* Notable changes included the upgrade of the balanced-crystalloids recommendation over normal saline, and a new recommendation on fluid strategy after the initial 30 mL/kg resuscitation that permits either a liberal or restrictive approach based on individual patient and health-system factors (the 2021 guidelines had reported insufficient evidence to recommend either way). For a substrate-first system that had been deployed in 2024 using SSC 2021 deduction operators, the transition through the substrate proceeds as follows.

The substrate's evidence-currency operator for the SSC 2021 set crosses its next-check-by threshold in March 2026, emitting a currency event (4D.2). The substrate registers the SSC 2026 operator set as a new version pair under 4D.1, with explicit registration timestamp, effective period, and a mapping table between SSC 2021 and SSC 2026 operators that flags 46 operators as new, the balanced-crystalloid operator as having an upgraded recommendation strength, and the post-30mL/kg fluid-strategy operator as substantively modified. The mapping itself is an audit-trail artifact captured with provenance.

Sound evolution under operator-set change (4D.3) runs across the mapping. The new operators are demonstrated not to license previously-rejected recommendations — the changes are upgrades and clarifications rather than contradictions, so no previously-rejected plan becomes silently permissible. The modified operators have their licensing differences tracked at the recommendation level. Where SSC 2021 specifically rejected a treatment that SSC 2026 now allows, that prior rejection is flagged for re-evaluation rather than absorbed; in the SSC 2026 case this is rare because the update primarily strengthens existing recommendations and clarifies under-specified ones.

Clinician-mediated propagation (4D.4) is where the architecture earns its safety property. The institution has 40 active sepsis patients on the transition day. For each, the substrate identifies all currently-active recommendations licensed under SSC 2021 operators. A patient currently on a normal-saline-based resuscitation plan licensed under SSC 2021 generates a re-review event for the responsible clinician: the SSC 2026 balanced-crystalloid recommendation is now licensed, and the clinician must affirmatively decide between transitioning the current patient to balanced crystalloid, continuing normal saline with documented justification (for example, specific contraindication, near-completion of resuscitation phase where transition adds risk without benefit, or pragmatic supply constraint), or holding the decision for the next clinical assessment. The architecture does not silently switch the active order. A patient who completed 30 mL/kg crystalloid yesterday under SSC 2021's "insufficient evidence" guidance for further fluid strategy, and is being assessed today for further fluid versus vasopressor escalation, generates a different re-review event: the new SSC 2026 operator permits either a liberal or a restrictive strategy with individual-factor reasoning, and the clinician's choice between them is now explicitly licensed rather than under-supported by guideline silence. This second re-review is not a change to existing care; it is a recognition that future decisions about this patient now have stronger guideline backing.

The institutional substrate (4B) absorbs the capacity implications. Forty active sepsis patients generate roughly forty re-review events on the transition day, plus continuing re-review events as new patients meeting prior-version criteria are admitted and assessed against the new operators. The institutional layer routes the events to appropriate clinicians without overwhelming the audit interface, paces them against clinical workflow priority, and surfaces the workload to capacity-management visibility (Section 7C) so administrators can make staffing decisions during the transition window. The cost of safety-by-construction is now a visible operational signal rather than an invisible drift.

Evolution-aware provenance (4D.5) captures the entire transition. Which patients had their plans transitioned to SSC 2026 operators and when. Which continued under documented SSC 2021 rationale and why. What the outcome correlations look like after the cohort completes its ICU stay. The institutional learning extends beyond compliance documentation: the institution can ask, six months later, whether outcomes in patients transitioned at any given point during the SSC 2026 rollout differed from those continuing on legacy operators, and whether specific operator transitions (balanced crystalloid, post-30mL/kg fluid strategy) correlated with outcome changes. This retrospective capacity is not in addition to the architecture; it is a structural consequence of versioned components, currency tracking, and propagation logging.

What this example illustrates: rolling evidence evolution is handled as a first-class architectural concern rather than as an operational afterthought patched into a deployed system. The system's behavior under guideline transition is specified, auditable, and safe-by-construction in the relevant sense — silent drift in active care is structurally impossible, the institutional cost of explicit propagation is itself visible to capacity management, and the architecture's safety claim under temporal evolution can be tested against actual outcome data rather than rested on operator assurance.

### Timeline predictions

The narrow applications are deployable on a 2–4 year horizon from a concrete start. *[generated]* Engineering — substrate specification, deduction operator authoring, learned-component training — is increasingly compressed by AI-accelerated tooling and is no longer the binding constraint. The binding constraints are clinical pilot validation, institutional adoption, and regulatory clearance, none of which accelerate at the same rate as engineering. The 2–4 year band reflects that asymmetry: the lower bound assumes a forward-leaning institution sponsors the pilot and the application has a fast-track regulatory pathway; the upper bound assumes both move at typical clinical-IT pace. The patient/institutional pairing does not extend this horizon for narrow applications because the institutional dimension is often already partially codified in existing hospital IT systems (formulary management, bed-tracking, pharmacy inventory) — the work is integrating those existing systems into the substrate's event interface, not building them from scratch. The temporal-evolution axis adds wiring work for FHIR Clinical Reasoning, MAGICapp currency feeds, PCCP framework alignment, and Article 72 monitoring obligations, but each of these is integration with existing infrastructure rather than from-scratch construction.

Broader diagnostic reasoning is a 5–8 year horizon. *[generated]* The substrate must be designed from first principles, the deduction operators are softer and require both expert specification and learned components under soundness constraints, and longitudinal patient state requires temporal lattice semantics for the patient-state axis that do not yet exist as published formalisms. (Distinguish carefully: "temporal" appears in three places in this document. Section 4A.1 covers short-term patient-state refinement during and across encounters. This line refers to long-term longitudinal patient state across weeks and months — disease progression, lab trajectory evolution, chronic-condition state — for which lattice formalisms do not yet exist. Section 4D covers substrate-knowledge evolution across months and years — guidelines updating, operators changing — for which standards exist and the architecture's job is to integrate with them. Same word, three different concerns; only the second remains an unsolved formalism problem.) The Cost-Driven Synthesis result (Gu et al., 17 Nov 2025) *[cited]* is the strongest current evidence that AI-assisted formal methods are maturing faster than a 2024 baseline would have predicted; if that trajectory holds, the substrate engineering portion compresses meaningfully — perhaps by half *[generated]*. The bottleneck is not engineering. It is clinical validation across enough specialties to demonstrate generalization, plus the regulatory work of establishing a substrate-as-device pathway rather than model-as-device.

The novelty-handling parts of medicine — rare diseases, presentations outside the current ontology, evolving evidence base, conditions whose lattice does not yet exist — are 8–12 years out under the most optimistic AI-acceleration trajectory, and longer under realistic conditions. *[generated]* The bottleneck is not the substrate or the model; it is formalizing ontology-expansion semantics that humans can audit and that preserve safety properties under expansion. This is research, not engineering, and AI acceleration compounds research timelines less reliably than it compounds engineering timelines. This phase happens only if the narrow and broader phases succeed first, both technically and in clinical acceptance.

Three things would compress these horizons further than the bands suggest: a precedent regulatory clearance for a substrate-architecture system in any high-safety domain (medical, aviation, autonomous vehicles) shifts the path for clinical AI; AI-accelerated formal methods tooling reaching the point where deduction-operator specification is near-automatic compresses substrate engineering by close to an order of magnitude; an obvious clinical-safety failure of a current-paradigm clinical LLM that triggers institutional risk re-assessment compresses adoption timelines. Three things would extend them: regulatory frameworks calcifying around model-as-device, ontology-maintenance failures preventing the substrate from staying current with evolving medicine, or clinical workflow incompatibility that the architecture cannot accommodate. The predictions above assume none of these but acknowledge their possibility; readers operating on a specific timeline should price in their own estimates of which factors apply to their context.

## 8. Honest limitations

This note does not claim the substrate-first architecture solves clinical reasoning. It claims that current architectures cannot solve it and that the substrate-first direction is the most promising candidate among the available options as of May 2026.

The claim depends on several assumptions that may turn out to be wrong:

That clinical reasoning is well-approximated by lattice-based partial-information refinement. This is true for diagnostic narrowing, medication selection, and decision-making within established guidelines. It may not be true for the parts of clinical practice that involve genuine novelty, narrative integration, or relational reasoning with the patient.

That sound deduction operators can be specified or learned with sufficient coverage to be clinically useful. The combinatorial space of clinical situations is large. The LDT result holds on closed-world benchmarks; clinical reasoning is open-world in important ways.

That ontologies like SNOMED CT, RxNorm, and LOINC are good enough substrates. They are good enough for the narrow applications listed above. They have known gaps for emerging conditions, rare presentations, and culturally-variable diagnostic categories.

That clinicians will accept a system whose strongest property is its refusal to do unsafe things. This is the cultural bet underneath the architectural bet. A system that abstains usefully looks different from a system that answers confidently, and the clinical workflow accommodation is not guaranteed.

That the design of justified-reversal semantics can resist becoming a back door that defeats the safety property. If reversal is too easy, the substrate's guarantees become advisory rather than enforced. If reversal is too restricted, the system becomes brittle on real clinical presentations that legitimately require reopening prior conclusions. Specifying which evidence licenses which reversal, under whose authority, and with which logged provenance is an unresolved design question. The first narrow application is the right place to find out whether the framing holds.

That the interaction semantics between patient and institutional substrates can be specified without producing an architectural seam that absorbs the safety properties of either layer. Cross-layer events have to carry enough provenance to support joint auditability without becoming a coupling point that requires the two substrates to share state representations they otherwise wouldn't. The risk is that interaction layer becomes the place where soundness fails — sound patient reasoning + sound institutional allocation + leaky interaction = unsound system. This is a real design problem that v0.6.0 names but does not solve.

That the institutional learned components — demand forecasting, queue dynamics, length-of-stay estimation — have soundness considerations distinct from clinical learned components and are not addressed in depth by this note. The capacity-aware ML literature has its own conventions for handling uncertainty, fairness, and distributional shift; integration with substrate-first principles requires engagement with that literature, which Section 6 names as queued work.

That the regulatory framing for two-layer systems is harder than for single-layer. A substrate-as-device argument was already non-standard; a substrate-as-device that includes institutional allocation crosses several existing regulatory categories (clinical decision support + capacity management + resource allocation infrastructure). The two-layer architecture might end up being easier to regulate than the patient-only version because it matches what hospitals actually deploy, or harder because it does not fit any single existing pathway cleanly. This is empirical and depends on engagement with regulators that has not happened.

That the four temporal-evolution standards Section 4D builds on — HL7 FHIR Clinical Reasoning and CQL, Cochrane Living Systematic Reviews and MAGICapp, FDA Predetermined Change Control Plans, and EU AI Act Article 72 post-market monitoring — are each individually mature and adopted in production, but their integration into a single deployed clinical substrate is engineering work that does not yet exist at scale. The 4D principles bet on what that integration should look like; they do not certify that it has been done. The engineering work for 4D is part of the 2–4 year deployment horizon Section 7 already names for narrow applications and is more significant than the Section 7 timeline narrative previously implied. If the integration proves harder than anticipated — if FHIR Clinical Reasoning resources do not capture the operator semantics the substrate requires, if living-guideline currency signals do not arrive at clinically useful cadence, if PCCP frameworks do not accommodate substrate-component versioning cleanly, or if Article 72 post-market monitoring requirements impose obligations the architecture cannot satisfy — the temporal-evolution principles may need to be revised. The principles are bets on integration tractability; the bets are not yet collected.

These limitations do not invalidate the position. They define the scope within which the position is testable. The narrow applications work; the broader applications are open research.

## 9. Invitation

This note exists to be argued with. The principles named in Section 4 are the load-bearing claims. The prior art mapped in Section 6 is the evidence the synthesis is currently unclaimed. The build sketched in Section 7 is the testable proposal.

The most useful responses, in order of preference:

A demonstration that the synthesis is already published and I missed it. The relevant principles individually have rich literatures; I have searched for the integration and not found it, but the search is bounded by what I can index.

A demonstration that one of the eighteen principles (five in 4A patient-state, five in 4B institutional-state, three in 4C interaction-semantics, five in 4D temporal-evolution) is wrong or unnecessary. The principles are stated separately so they can be attacked separately.

A demonstration that the substrate-first framing fails on a narrow clinical application where it should work. The first failure mode of the architecture, on the easiest application, is the most informative result.

A proposal to build the first narrow application. If the architecture is right, antimicrobial stewardship or drug-drug interaction reasoning is the first place to find out.

Disagreements that arrive after this draft is timestamped and become the next version's revisions are how the position gets sharper. Disagreements that arrive in private and do not become part of the public record are useful but slower.
